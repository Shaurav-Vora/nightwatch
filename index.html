NIGHTWATCH

Heat maps measure the wrong thing.

Cities decide where to plant trees and open cooling centres using maps of how hot it gets. Across a whole city that barely changes. How long it stays dangerous changes enormously — and the same neighbourhoods lose every time.

Open the map → See the evidence first

The problem

A whole city fits inside two degrees. Nobody acts on two degrees.

We measured every 100-metre block across 21 square miles of Phoenix on a hot July afternoon. From the coolest block to the hottest, the entire spread was 1.8 °C. Look at that map and nothing demands attention.

Then we asked the same blocks a different question: how many hours in a row do you spend above the danger threshold?

Two blocks in Phoenix both read 99.7 °F at 3pm. One spends 6.1 hours above 35 °C. The other spends 11.2. Five hours apart, identical on a temperature map.

The obvious objection

“That’s just one bad afternoon.”

It was the first thing we tested. We re-ran the whole measurement across five separate heat events between 2022 and 2025, then counted how often each block landed in the worst quarter of its city.

If the worst blocks moved around with the weather, almost none would appear every time. Some cities behave that way. Others don’t.

In Houston and Chicago the same ground loses every time — that is a property of the built environment, not of the weather. In Phoenix it is far weaker, and the site list there should not be treated as fixed. We report all three.

What we got wrong

The idea we started with turned out to be false.

The original plan was to show that the hottest block at 3pm is not the most dangerous block at 3am. Chicago agreed emphatically — a correlation of −0.73.

It was Lake Michigan. Exclude everything within 3 km of water and it flips to +0.13. Test two cities with no lake and it disappears entirely: Houston came back at +0.84, meaning afternoon heat predicts night heat almost perfectly.

So we abandoned it and kept looking. The finding above is what survived three progressively stricter tests. The failed one is still in the commit history, because a result nobody tried to break isn’t worth much.

How it works

Built on FortyGuard’s temperature API.

  • Exposure comes from analytic_type: persistence — the longest unbroken run of hours above a danger threshold, not a scattered total. Eight consecutive dangerous hours is an event; eight scattered ones are not.
  • Temperatures are two tcm calls at 3pm and 3am local time. We established the API speaks local rather than UTC by sweeping all 24 hours and finding the pre-dawn minimum — the documentation says otherwise.
  • Thresholds are set per city, between its own measured afternoon and pre-dawn means, so a mild city and an extreme one are both measurable.
  • Sites are joined-up patches of blocks that were worst on every heat event, exportable as CSV and GeoJSON for a planning department.
