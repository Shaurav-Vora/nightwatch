NIGHTWATCH

The heat maps cities use today measure the wrong thing.

NIGHTWATCH is an interactive map of how many hours a day each city block spends above its danger threshold, for Phoenix, Houston and Chicago. It names the neighbourhoods that stay dangerous longest, shows that the same ones come out worst on hot day after hot day, and ranks them by how many people live there.

Built for the people who decide where a city spends its cooling budget. 53,000 blocks measured, at 100 metres, using FortyGuard's temperature API.

Three cities

Three cities that behave differently, each with its own threshold.

An arid city with no water, a humid sprawling one, and a lake-dominated one. Each map below is hours spent above that city's danger threshold, on 22 July 2025.

Where the threshold comes from

The threshold is the temperature a block has to stay above to count as dangerous. We set it per city, midway between that city's own measured 3pm and 3am means:

City3pm mean3am mean MidpointThreshold used
Phoenix38.33 °C31.31 °C 34.82 °C35 °C
Houston36.20 °C27.29 °C 31.75 °C32 °C
Chicago29.99 °C20.74 °C 25.36 °C25 °C

A single fixed number across all three would measure nothing. At 35 °C Chicago never crosses the threshold and every block scores zero. At 25 °C Phoenix never drops below it and every block scores twenty-four. The midpoint guarantees the day partly clears the threshold, and that is the only place duration has room to vary.

What the hours number counts

One call to the API covers a full 24 hours and returns the longest unbroken stretch above the threshold. Here is that day in Chicago, so you can see where the stretch falls.

It runs 09:00 to 22:00, so the measured stretch covers the afternoon and ends about two hours after sunset. By 3am Chicago is back near 21 °C, four degrees below its own threshold, which is why the 3am layer has no block above the threshold anywhere in the app.

Blocks differ at both ends of that stretch. Some cross the threshold at 09:00 and others at 11:00; some drop back below it at 20:00 and others hold on until 22:00. That is where the spread in hours comes from.

So compare within a city, not between them. Because each city has its own threshold, the hour figures are not interchangeable: Chicago's 11.6-hour spread is not "worse" than Houston's 5.6-hour spread, it is measured against a different threshold. What is comparable across cities is R², which has no units, and the repeat ratios further down. Every export carries its threshold_c in the file so the number can never travel without it.

Why it matters

A whole city fits inside two degrees. Nobody acts on two degrees.

We measured every 100-metre block across 83 square miles of Phoenix on a hot July afternoon. From the coolest block to the hottest, the entire spread was 1.8 °C. Nothing on that map asks for your attention.

Then we asked the same blocks a different question: how many hours in a row do you spend above the danger threshold?

Two blocks in Phoenix both read 99.7 °F at 3pm. One spends 6.1 hours above 35 °C. The other spends 11.2. Five hours apart, identical on a temperature map.

Heat duration, not peak temperature, is what correlates with excess mortality in the epidemiological literature. A body that never gets to cool overnight never recovers.

The obvious objection

"That's just one bad afternoon."

It was the first thing we tested. We re-ran the whole measurement across five separate hot days between 2022 and 2025, then counted how often each block landed in the worst quarter of its city. Houston kept all five dates. Phoenix and Chicago kept three, because on the others the city was either saturated or too cool to tell blocks apart.

If the worst blocks moved around with the weather, almost none would appear every time. Some cities behave that way. Others don't.

In Houston and Chicago the same ground is worst every time. That is a property of the built environment, not of the weather. In Phoenix it is far weaker, and the site list there should not be treated as fixed. We report all three.

How it works

Built on FortyGuard's temperature API.

  • Exposure comes from analytic_type: persistence: the longest unbroken run of hours above a danger threshold, not a scattered total. Eight consecutive dangerous hours is an event; eight scattered ones are not.
  • Temperatures are two tcm calls at 3pm and 3am local time. We established the API speaks local rather than UTC by sweeping all 24 hours and finding the pre-dawn minimum. The documentation says otherwise.
  • Thresholds are 35 °C in Phoenix, 32 °C in Houston and 25 °C in Chicago, each midway between that city's own measured afternoon and pre-dawn means. The derivation and the comparability caveat are in Three cities above.
  • Sites are joined-up patches of blocks that were worst on every hot day, exportable as CSV and GeoJSON for a planning department.
