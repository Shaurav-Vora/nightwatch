NIGHTWATCH - AUDIO SCRIPT
=========================

Read straight through. Nothing here is a stage direction, so you can record
it in one take without looking at the app.

The bracketed pauses are where the video will cut, so hold them properly.
Two seconds is longer than it feels; count it. The one marked THREE is the
important one, because that is the shot where the afternoon map sits on
screen and the viewer needs time to look at it before you speak again.

Pace is about 150 words a minute, unhurried. The whole read is 392 words,
roughly 2 minutes 55 including the pauses.

The timestamps on the left are where each block should land. Use them to cut
the footage afterwards; they are not instructions to speed up or slow down.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:00
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: the landing page, top. Title and the opening paragraph)

Cities decide where to plant trees and where to open cooling centres using
maps of how hot it gets. NIGHTWATCH maps something else: how many hours a day
each city block spends above its danger threshold. Phoenix, Houston and
Chicago, block by block.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:20
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: the landing page, the two Phoenix maps side by side, both
      fully on screen)

Here is why that matters. Same blocks, same afternoon, asked two ways. On the
left, how hot it got. On the right, how long it stayed dangerous. Take the
hundred hottest at three p.m. and the hundred worst for duration: in Phoenix
not one block is on both lists. Choose sites from the left map and you miss
all hundred on the right.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:45
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: Phoenix, the "Hours dangerously hot" layer)

This is the app. Phoenix, one day in July. The colour is how many hours
each block spent above ninety-five Fahrenheit. Red is longest.

     [pause TWO - the map switches to "Afternoon heat"]

     (picture: same city, same day, now coloured by temperature)

Now the same blocks, same afternoon, as temperature. The map a city uses
today. It looks dramatic, but read the scale: the whole city fits inside three
degrees.

     [pause THREE - hold on the temperature map, this is the one the viewer
      needs time to look at]

     (picture: back to "Hours dangerously hot")

And back to hours. Under three degrees of temperature. Eight hours of
exposure. The temperature map cannot separate these blocks. The hours map
separates them by more than double.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1:22
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: the app, right-hand panel, the "Two blocks, same afternoon"
      card, held long enough to read the coordinates)

The app searches Phoenix for two blocks that read the same temperature at
three in the afternoon. These two both read ninety-nine point seven
Fahrenheit, so a temperature map cannot tell them apart. One spends six hours
above the danger threshold, the other eleven. Five hours, at the same
temperature.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1:42
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: "Hottest areas, for longest". Click the top row so the map
      flies there and outlines it, then scroll on to "Who lives there")

This is the output. Joined-up patches of blocks that stayed worst on every
hot day, with coordinates, ordered by residents rather than by size. The top
one holds three thousand four hundred people and is only the fourth largest. The largest of
the nine holds seven hundred and ninety-seven. Rank by size and you start in
the wrong place.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2:14
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: Houston, on "Hottest every time" so the stamp lists all five
      dates, then the "Does it repeat?" card showing 1,408 and 64.6x)

The obvious objection is one bad afternoon, so we tested five hot days
between twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-five.
In Houston, fourteen hundred blocks landed in the hottest quarter of the city
every single time. Chance would give you twenty-two. That is sixty-five times
over. That is not weather. That is the built environment, and a city can
change it.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2:42
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

     (picture: the download buttons, then "Generate full report" opening the
      printable page. Scroll it once)

All of it exports. Ranked sites as a spreadsheet, the grid as GeoJSON, and a
printable report carrying the method and the limitations. Built on
FortyGuard's API, three cities, fifty-three thousand blocks. Heat maps measure
how hot. This measures how long.

     [stop recording a beat after the last word, do not clip it]


=========================================================================
SAYING IT
=========================================================================

Numbers, said the way they are written here:

  "three p.m."                  three pee em, not fifteen hundred
  "ninety-nine point seven"     not ninety nine seven
  "twenty twenty-two"           the year, not two thousand and twenty two
  "sixty-five times over"       the ratio is 64.6, so sixty-five is right
  "fifty-three thousand"        53,392 blocks across the three cities
  "GeoJSON"                     gee-oh-jay-son

Two places people stumble:

  "Under three degrees of temperature. Eight hours of exposure." - these are
  the two halves of the whole argument. Slow down and separate them.

  "three thousand four hundred people" and "seven hundred and ninety-seven" -
  these two numbers are the point of that beat. Land them both cleanly.

If you fluff a line, stop, breathe, and take that whole block again from its
timestamp. Do not patch mid-sentence; the join always shows.


=========================================================================
IF THE READ COMES OUT LONG
=========================================================================

Cut in this order. Each one is a whole sentence, so nothing is left dangling.

  1. The last sentence of 1:22, "Five hours, at the same temperature."
     The card on screen already shows both figures.

  2. The opening clause of 0:00, so it starts at "NIGHTWATCH maps something
     else".

  3. The last sentence of 1:42, "Rank by size and you start in the wrong
     place."

Do not cut anything from 0:45. That block is the argument.
