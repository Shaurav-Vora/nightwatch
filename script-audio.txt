NIGHTWATCH - AUDIO SCRIPT
=========================

Read straight through. Nothing here is a stage direction, so you can record
it in one take without looking at the app.

The bracketed pauses are where the video will cut, so hold them properly.
Two seconds is longer than it feels; count it. The one marked THREE is the
important one, because that is the shot where the afternoon map sits on
screen and the viewer needs time to look at it before you speak again.

Pace is about 150 words a minute, unhurried. The whole read is 351 words,
roughly 2 minutes 40 including the pauses.

The timestamps on the left are where each block should land. Use them to cut
the footage afterwards; they are not instructions to speed up or slow down.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:00
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cities decide where to plant trees and where to open cooling centres using
maps of how hot it gets. NIGHTWATCH maps something else: how many hours a day
each city block spends above its danger threshold. Phoenix, Houston and
Chicago, block by block.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:20
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here is why that matters. Same city, same afternoon, same blocks, asked two
ways. On the left, how hot it got. On the right, how long it stayed
dangerous. Take the hundred hottest blocks at three p.m., and the hundred
that stayed dangerous longest. In Phoenix, not one block is on both lists.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
0:45
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the live version, on one single day so the comparison is fair. Red is
the ground that stayed dangerous longest. Here is the map a city would use
today.

     [pause THREE - the afternoon map is on screen and needs looking at]

Temperature at three that same afternoon. The colours look dramatic, so read
the scale: just under a hundred, up to a hundred and two Fahrenheit. Three
degrees across the whole city.

     [pause TWO - the map switches back]

And back to hours above that threshold. The same blocks, the same day, and now
the scale reads six hours to fourteen.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1:22
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

What that looks like on the ground. These two blocks both read ninety-nine
point seven Fahrenheit at three in the afternoon, so a temperature map cannot
tell them apart. One spends six hours above the danger threshold. The other
eleven.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
1:42
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the output. Joined-up areas that stayed worst on every hot day, with
coordinates. They are ordered by how many people live in them, not by size.
Look at the last column on each row: the area with the most residents is only
the fourth largest, and the largest of the nine ranks fifth for people. Rank
by area, which is what a city would otherwise do, and you send the money to a
different place.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2:14
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

The obvious objection is that this is one bad afternoon, so we tested that
first, across five hot days between twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-five.
In Houston, fourteen hundred blocks landed in the worst quarter of the city
every single time. Chance would give you twenty-two. That is sixty-five times
over, so this is the built environment rather than the weather, and someone
can fix it.

     [pause TWO]


-------------------------------------------------------------------------
2:42
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

All of it exports. Ranked sites as a spreadsheet, the grid as GeoJSON, and a
printable report carrying the method and the limitations. Built on
FortyGuard's API, three cities, fifty-three thousand blocks. Heat maps measure
how hot. This measures how long.

     [stop recording a beat after the last word, do not clip it]


=========================================================================
SAYING IT
=========================================================================

Numbers, said the way they are written here:

  "three p.m."                  three pee em, not fifteen hundred
  "ninety-nine point seven"     not ninety nine seven
  "twenty twenty-two"           the year, not two thousand and twenty two
  "sixty-five times over"       the ratio is 64.6, so sixty-five is right
  "fifty-three thousand"        53,392 blocks across the three cities
  "GeoJSON"                     gee-oh-jay-son

Two places people stumble:

  "on one single day so the comparison is fair" - keep going, it is one
  clause, not two.

  "the largest of the nine ranks fifth for people" - the stress is on fifth.

If you fluff a line, stop, breathe, and take that whole block again from its
timestamp. Do not patch mid-sentence; the join always shows.


=========================================================================
IF THE READ COMES OUT LONG
=========================================================================

Cut in this order. Each one is a whole sentence, so nothing is left dangling.

  1. The last sentence of 1:22, "One spends six hours above the danger
     threshold. The other eleven." The card is on screen and says it.

  2. The opening clause of 0:00, so it starts at "NIGHTWATCH maps something
     else".

  3. The last sentence of 1:42, "Rank by area, which is what a city would
     otherwise do, and you send the money to a different place."

Do not cut anything from 0:45. That block is the argument.
