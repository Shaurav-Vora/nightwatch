NIGHTWATCH - 3 MINUTE DEMO SCRIPT
=================================

Read the NARRATION lines aloud. Do what the ACTION lines say while you read
them. Pace is about 150 words a minute, which is unhurried. The cut list is
at the bottom if you run long.


BEFORE YOU RECORD
-----------------
[ ] Open https://shaurav-vora.github.io/nightwatch/ in a clean window. No
    bookmarks bar, no extensions showing.
[ ] Browser zoom 100%. Window at least 1600 wide, or the three columns of the
    app will stack.
[ ] Load both pages once, then reload. Cold tiles look like lag on camera.
[ ] On the map page, click Skip on the tour. You are the tour.
[ ] Leave it on Phoenix, layer "Hottest every time", filter at 100%.
[ ] Record at 1080p or better. Move the mouse slower than feels natural.


0:00 - 0:20   WHAT IT IS
-------------
ACTION   Landing page, top. Hold on the title and the opening line.

NARRATION
  Cities decide where to plant trees and where to open cooling centres using
  maps of how hot it gets. NIGHTWATCH maps something else: how many hours each
  city block stays dangerously hot after dark. Phoenix, Houston and Chicago,
  block by block.


0:20 - 0:45   THE TWO MAPS
-------------
ACTION   Scroll to the pair of Phoenix maps. Let both sit on screen while you
         talk. Point at the left one, then the right one.

NARRATION
  Here is why that matters. Same city, same afternoon, same blocks, asked two
  ways. On the left, how hot it got. On the right, how long it stayed
  dangerous. If the first told you where the danger was, these two pictures
  would match. They do not even look like the same city.


0:45 - 1:10   THE TOGGLE
-------------
ACTION   Click "Open the map". Wait for Phoenix to draw.
         Click "Afternoon heat". Hold two seconds.
         Click back to "Hottest every time".

NARRATION
  This is the live version. Red is the ground that stays dangerous longest.
  Now here is the map a city would use today.
  [PAUSE on Afternoon heat]
  Temperature at three in the afternoon. Almost flat. Back to duration.
  [CLICK BACK]
  A different city. In Phoenix, how hot a block gets in the afternoon tells you
  almost nothing about its night. R-squared is zero point one.


1:10 - 1:30   TWO REAL BLOCKS
-------------
ACTION   Scroll the right panel to the twin blocks card. Hold long enough for
         the coordinates to be readable.

NARRATION
  What that looks like on the ground. These two blocks both read ninety-nine
  point seven Fahrenheit at three in the afternoon, so a temperature map cannot
  tell them apart. One spends six hours above the danger line. The other spends
  eleven.


1:30 - 1:55   DOES IT REPEAT
-------------
ACTION   Click "Houston" in the bar above the map. Scroll the right panel to
         the card showing 1,408 and 64.6x.

NARRATION
  The obvious objection is that this was one bad afternoon, so we tested that
  first, across five heat events between twenty twenty-two and twenty
  twenty-five. In Houston, fourteen hundred blocks landed in the worst quarter
  of the city every single time. Chance would give you twenty-two. That is
  sixty-five times over. This is the built environment, not the weather, which
  means someone can fix it.


1:55 - 2:18   WHO LIVES THERE
-------------
ACTION   Scroll to the population card, then the ranked list. Click the top
         row and let the map fly and outline the site.

NARRATION
  Then we joined census population. Twenty-nine thousand people live in that
  core, and the density there is below the city average, because the hottest
  ground is mostly industrial. So the list is ordered by residents rather than
  by area, and it shows you which sites move when you do that.


2:18 - 2:40   THE PART THAT FAILED
-------------
ACTION   Switch to the landing page tab. Scroll to "What we got wrong".

NARRATION
  One more thing. We set out to show that the hottest block at three p.m. is
  the safest at three a.m. Chicago agreed. It was Lake Michigan. Take out the
  land within three kilometres of water and it flips. We dropped it, and the
  failed version is still in the repo, because a result nobody attacked is
  worth very little.


2:40 - 3:00   WHAT YOU CAN DO WITH IT
-------------
ACTION   Back to the map tab. Click "Generate full report". Let it open and
         scroll it once. End on the report or the map.

NARRATION
  All of it exports. Ranked sites as a spreadsheet, the grid as GeoJSON, and a
  printable report carrying the method and the limitations. Built on
  FortyGuard's API, three cities, fifty-three thousand blocks. Heat maps measure
  how hot. This measures how long.


IF YOU RUN LONG
---------------
Cut in this order:
  1. The last sentence of 1:10, about the six and eleven hours. The card is on
     screen and says it.
  2. The whole 2:18 beat. It costs you the honesty point, so cut it last.
  3. Start 0:00 at "NIGHTWATCH maps something else".

Do not cut the toggle at 0:45. That toggle is the argument, and the video does
not work without it.


NUMBERS, IF A JUDGE ASKS
------------------------
  Phoenix   1.81 C spread  ->  9.07 h spread   R2 0.106   21,453 blocks
  Houston   4.20 C spread  ->  5.56 h spread   R2 0.802   22,333 blocks
  Chicago   5.56 C spread  -> 11.56 h spread   R2 0.783    9,606 blocks

  Thresholds are per city, midway between that city's own 3pm and 3am means:
    Phoenix 35 C, Houston 32 C, Chicago 25 C. Hours compare within a city,
    not between cities. R2 is the number that compares.

  Repeat:   Houston 1,408 blocks worst every time = 64.6x chance
            Chicago 1,298 = 10.2x
            Phoenix   817 =  2.4x, which is weak, and the app says so

  Twins:    both 37.63 C at 3pm, 6.10 h vs 11.18 h
  Lake:     Chicago rho -0.731 across all tiles, +0.130 beyond 3 km of water
            Houston +0.842, no lake
